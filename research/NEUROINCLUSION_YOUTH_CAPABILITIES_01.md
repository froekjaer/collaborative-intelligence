# Neuroinclusion and youth capabilities — research track 01

**Status:** Proposed research extension, 2026-11-15; not a validated employment intervention, clinical guidance, or canonical Mission Framework policy.  
**Parent:** [Collaborative Intelligence v2 foundations](./COLLABORATIVE_INTELLIGENCE_V2_FOUNDATIONS.md).

## Proposition

Young people with diagnoses, neurodivergent young people and young people with different functional profiles may be excluded by inflexible educational, recruitment and work environments despite having useful individual capabilities. This is a **testable, heterogeneous proposition**, not a claim that a diagnosis predicts talent or that everyone should pursue conventional employment.

## Human-first principles

- **Person before label:** Ask about the person's own aspirations, interests, skills, communication preferences, support needs and desired participation; diagnosis is neither a talent certificate nor an exclusion criterion.
- **Two-way adaptation:** Match and adapt tasks, environments, mentoring and tools to the person; do not assume the person alone must conform to a fixed workflow.
- **Strengths and support coexist:** A person can have exceptional strengths in some contexts and substantial assistance needs in others. Never romanticise disability as a 'superpower' or demand exceptional productivity as the price of inclusion.
- **Self-determination:** Participation, disclosure of diagnosis, use of AI, mentoring and sharing of personal information must be voluntary and appropriately supported. For minors, apply age-appropriate safeguards and legitimate guardian/legal requirements without silencing the young person's voice.
- **No diagnosis-based profiling:** Do not infer competence, reliability, risk or employment suitability from diagnostic categories. Assess relevant skills through accessible, consent-based methods.
- **Fairness and dignity:** Preserve reasonable accommodations, accessibility, compensation, non-discrimination, privacy and meaningful avenues to contest decisions. A successful outcome may be education, belonging, wellbeing, voluntary contribution or paid work — defined with the participant.
- **Bidirectional Reality Belt:** Measure lived wellbeing and actual opportunity, not merely placements, process completion or output counts; adapt institutions when observations contradict assumptions.

## Example research settings (hypotheses, not stereotyped role assignments)

- Accessible software quality assurance, documentation, research and creative tasks with different communication and sensory arrangements.
- Technical apprenticeships with senior mentors, reciprocal learning and optional AI scaffolding.
- Flexible scheduling, asynchronous participation, clear task boundaries, assistive tools and supported transitions.
- Alternative ways to demonstrate skills beyond conventional interviews, timed tests or credential proxies.

No diagnostic group is assigned a presumed occupational niche. Tasks must be co-designed with young people and relevant support practitioners.

## Testable questions

1. Does **individualised strengths-and-support assessment** improve self-reported belonging, sustained participation and task quality compared with standard one-size-fits-all allocation?
2. Do **environmental and workflow adaptations** improve outcomes more than attempting to change only the individual?
3. Does AI assistance increase independent capability after withdrawal, or cause dependence and skill erosion?
4. Can intergenerational mentoring increase both younger participants' opportunities and older mentors' learning without increasing workload or paternalism?
5. Are access, retention, wellbeing and remuneration equitable across gender, diagnosis/disability status, socioeconomic background and other relevant intersections, with privacy-preserving analysis?
6. Do participants retain meaningful choice to refuse roles, stop participation or change goals without penalty?

**Comparator:** accessible conventional support and mentorship, not a deliberately weak control. **Endpoints:** participant-defined goals, wellbeing, autonomy, sustained opportunity, delayed independent skill, quality of work, support burden, discrimination incidents, privacy harms and actual environmental adaptations. Report distributions and adverse effects; never collapse all into a placement count.

## Ethical and operational safeguards

- Co-design with neurodivergent and disabled young people, families where appropriate, educators, employers, disability-rights advocates and relevant clinicians; no single group speaks for everyone.
- Obtain appropriate independent ethics review and informed, accessible consent/assent before involving participants; provide withdrawal and complaints mechanisms.
- Avoid collecting medical diagnoses unless strictly necessary, lawful and proportionate; separate support preferences from medical data and minimise retention.
- Do not expose health data to AI vendors or use opaque AI screening to rank individuals. Human accountable decisions, accessibility and appeal are required.
- Monitor fatigue, sensory load, masking pressures, bullying, stigma and mentor burnout. Provide alternatives to employment-centric success.
- Disclose selection bias, small sample sizes, confounders and differences between neurodiversity identity, clinical diagnosis and disability law.

## Scholarly grounding required before intervention claims

Investigate, critically and with exact-edition verification:
- WHO *International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF)* (2001) for person–environment interaction.
- UN *Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities* (2006), especially education, work and participation; normative instrument, not efficacy study.
- Disability studies' **social model** and critiques of simplistic social/medical binaries.
- Neurodiversity scholarship including Judy Singer and later critical disability/neurodiversity authors; do not assume one voice represents all.
- Supported employment, transition-to-adulthood, universal design for learning, participatory design, assistive technology and human–AI skill retention systematic reviews.
- Critiques of deficit-only and strengths-only framings; intersectional gender and socioeconomic analyses.
